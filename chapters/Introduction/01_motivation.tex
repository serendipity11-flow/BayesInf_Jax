\section{Motivation} 
\label{sec:introduction:motivation}

\begin{highlightbox}
In this seemingly certain world, uncertainty lingers like a shadow, yet from the uncertain, we often find certainty.       
\end{highlightbox}  

The paradoxical relationship between certainty and uncertainty manifests across disciplines, demonstrating how truth often arises from navigating ambiguity. Quantum mechanics redefines predictability under randomness in the subatomic world, where Newtonian deterministic physics collapses. This uncertainty led to groundbreaking laws (e.g. Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle), which now underpin modern technologies like quantum computing and all forms of spectroscopy. An example of medical diagnosis is that a patient presents vague symptoms, such as fatigue or pain, which could indicate anything from stress to cancer. Doctors converge on the cause through iterative hypothesis-testing approaches. Climate science also epitomizes how we manage uncertainty. Climate models incorporate a wide range of sources of variability and uncertainty. A common way to address it is to combine the results of a collection of different simulation models. By running multi-model ensembles, scientists can identify consistent patterns across the uncertainty.

From quantum randomness to medical diagnosis to climate models, uncertainty does not destroy the deterministic systems, but provides us accessibility to identify the systems. This perspective supports the use of probabilistic inference methods, which offer a rigorous framework for representing uncertainty and reasoning from uncertainty. In statistics, modeling uncertainty of variables from given data samples, using a probability distribution rather than just a point estimate is known as inference. The estimated distribution facilitates uncertainty quantification and provides a measure of reliability of the results, making it particularly well-suited for real-world applications. 

Probabilistic inference grounded in Bayes' rule is known as {\bfseries\itshape Bayesian inference}. Bayes' rule interprets unknown random variables with beliefs (the prior). The beliefs are updated using observed data (likelihood), yielding the posterior distribution. A simplified formulation is:
\begin{equation*}
    \label{eq:bayes_sim}
    \text{posterior} = \text{prior}\times \text{likelihood} \;/\; \text{total evidence (or marginal likelihood)}.
\end{equation*}
For example, we can illustrate Bayes' rule using the medical diagnosis scenario: the objective is to obtain the probability of a disease given a positive test result:
\begin{align*}
& P(\text{Disease} | \text{Positive}) = \frac{P(\text{Positive} | \text{Disease}) \times P(\text{Disease})}{P(\text{Positive})},\\
& P(\text{Positive}) = P(\text{Positive} | \text{Disease}) \times P(\text{Disease}) + P(\text{Positive} | \overline{\text{Disease}}) \times P(\overline{\text{Disease}}),
\end{align*}
where $\overline{\text{Disease}}$ denotes "No Disease".
In a more general Bayesian context, inference is the process of obtaining the posterior distribution of target variables from observed data. This perspective extends naturally to modern machine learning, where unknown values, structures, and policies can all be understood as random variables and probability distributions \citep{pml1Book}.

Especially in modern \acfp{DGM}, Bayesian inference plays an essential role \citep{pml2Book}. Through introducing random variables to represent complex data in latent space, \acp{DGM} enable data synthesis from latent variables (generation) based on the recognition of underlying latent representation from data (inference). However, directly inferring the posterior distribution over the latent variables using Bayes' rule \ref{eq:bayes} is generally intractable, since all possible values of the latent variables are rarely obtainable in complex and high-dimensional models. Addressing this challenge is essential to fully unfold the potential of \acp{DGM}. \acfp{VAE} \citep{Kingma_2019} tackle this problem by introducing an inference network as a tractable approximation to the posterior. \acfp{NF} \citep{rezende2016} construct bijective transformations between observed data and latent variables, achieving exact computation of posteriors. Diffusion models \citep{ho2020ddpm} sidestep calculating the posterior distribution over latent variables by designing it as a simple Gaussian, and instead frame both inference and generation as an iterative denoising process from noise through learned transformations.

To sum up, \acp{DGM} are inherently probabilistic; through probabilistic modeling and inference, they are powerful in extracting latent information and representing uncertainty in complex data. These capabilities are proven in numerous applications in images, text, and speech. The same principles extend naturally to scientific and engineering domains. One prominent example is: how can \acp{DGM} empower identification and modeling of complex, stochastic, nonlinear dynamical systems? In control theory, the process of building mathematical models of dynamical systems from observed input-output data is called {\bfseries\itshape system identification}. Interpreting system identification in probabilistic terms, dynamics modeling is inferring the posterior of the next states based on the previous states and system inputs, while parameter identification is estimating the posterior over system parameters given observations. Identifying system in probabilistic way enables uncertainty quantification, which is crucial for reliable and robust decision-making in real-world applications.

Classic methods for system identification rely on linearized models or pre-specified nonlinear structures. Model parameters are then estimated from observed data using estimation techniques. Subsequently, a validation process is employed to assess and select the most appropriate model structure. Although these methods offer strong physical interpretability and are often data-efficient, they exhibit several limitations: parameter estimation can be computationally inefficient with large amounts of data; results are sensitive to the choice of model structures and dependent on expert knowledge; uncertainty is difficult to handle and quantify; and they are generally inapplicable to partially observable systems and high-dimensional and raw data. 

Despite the demand for uncertainty quantification, deep learning methods are suitable for system identification thanks to their strength in representing information from complex data, flexibility of function approximation, scalability in high-dimensional data, and compatibility with optimization techniques. Taking uncertainty quantification into account, a simple yet effective technique is Gaussian Process Regression \citep{sarkka2019gp}, which involves assigning a Gaussian process prior to the latent regressor and performing statistical conditioning based on the available observations. This method has gained widespread adoption due to its non-parametric characteristic and adaptability. Nonetheless, its significant computational and memory requirements limit its feasibility when dealing with large datasets. Another straightforward approach to combining the advantages of deep learning methods and the possibility of uncertainty modeling is the \acf{BNN} \citep{Goan_2020}. Unlike standard neural networks that produce point estimates, \acp{BNN} assign probability distributions over the network parameters, thereby inducing distributions for outputs. However, \acp{BNN} reveal expressiveness limitations because the approximate inference may not reliably capture true posterior behavior \citep{foong2020bnn}. Therefore, the adaptation and application of \acp{DGM} are essential for achieving rich representational capacity, flexible modeling frameworks, and the ability to effectively handle large-scale data. 

Different \acp{DGM} have been investigated in system identification applications. \acp{VAE} are flexible to cooperate with time series modeling architectures such as \acf{RNN} \citep{Girin_2021} and \acf{NARX} \citep{PANIAGUA2024200344}, which is beneficial in uncovering the time-dependent features from data. However, \acp{VAE} suffers from a well-known problem of shrinkage of variance \citep{asperti2020vae} and tends to converge to a degenerate local optimum known as posterior collapse \citep{wang2023vae}. Unlike \acp{VAE}, \acp{NF} are restricted in their architectural choices due to the requirement of invertibility, which limits their direct applicability to time series modeling. On the other side, \acp{NF} provide exact and expressive density estimation capabilities, qualifying them for capturing uncertainty in data and enhancing the representational capacity of probabilistic forecasts. Diffusion models release the architectural constraints and manifest the strength in generating high-quality and coherent predictions. Nevertheless, their iterative data generation procedure incurs substantial computational overhead. Balancing expressiveness, computational efficiency, and sample quality, {\bfseries\itshape normalizing flows} are chosen in this work as the generative backbone of probabilistic system identification.

Based on the results of system identification, {\bfseries\itshape state estimation} is the process of inferring the value of hidden, time-varying state variables of a dynamical system from a history of noisy and potentially partial observations and known control inputs. Because of the noise in the sensors (measurement noise) and unmodeled disturbances in the system itself (process noise), state estimation is naturally probabilistic. Thus, estimating the target states is computing the posterior distribution of target states. The traditional method for state estimation is the Luenberger State Observer, which assumes the systems are deterministic and both the system dynamics and measurement models are linear and time-invariant. Taking stochasticity into account, Kalman Filter is the most widely applied method. Beyond system linearity, a Kalman Filter assumes the process and measurement noises are additive Gaussian noise with zero mean. In order to handle nonlinear systems, Extended Kalman Filter linearizes the nonlinear functions around the state estimate. However, the linearization introduces errors, leading to wrong estimation or even divergence. An improved modification is the Unscented Kalman Filter, which carefully selects the means and variances and propagates them through the nonlinear functions. However, it is still only compatible with Gaussian noises.

Through relaxing the need for precise modeling of dynamical systems and avoiding restrictive assumptions about uncertainty---while preserving expressiveness, modeling flexibility, and scalability to large datasets---the chosen architecture for probabilistic system identification, \ac{NF}, also provides a foundation for accurate, efficient and reliable probabilistic state estimation. From a data-centric perspective, parameter identification involves mapping time series to discrete data points; state estimation entails inferring data points or reconstructing time series from temporal data. In order to address these tasks, it is necessary to extend normalizing flows, given the limited research currently available in this area. Bridging this gap not only broadens the applicability of \acp{NF} but also contributes to advancing Bayesian inference techniques for complex temporal systems.

Having explored system identification and state estimation through Bayesian inference, we can advance toward {\bfseries\itshape optimal control} by leveraging data as a central source of insight into system behavior, enabling more adaptive, scalable, and robust control strategies. This is especially advantageous in situations where analytical models are incomplete, uncertain, or impractical to derive. One unmediated application is model-free control, which obtains control inputs directly from observations and reference trajectories without requiring an explicit system model. This enables fast and flexible path planning and decision-making, particularly when multiple samples or rollouts are required for real-time path planning and tracking tasks. Building upon model-free principles, \ac{RL} extends this paradigm by optimizing control policies through interaction with the environment. \ac{RL} agents learn to maximize long-term rewards while adapting to system uncertainties and disturbances, effectively extend the capability of traditional \ac{MPC}. By combining Bayesian inference with data-driven exploration, \ac{RL} offers a powerful framework for achieving uncertainty-aware, robust, and self-improving control in dynamic environments.

In summary, this work is motivated by three key considerations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bayesian inference provides a principled framework for modeling uncertainty and extracting knowledge from uncertainty, enabling system identification and state estimation based solely on observations. This is particularly valuable in scenarios where first-principles models are unavailable, incomplete, or prohibitively complex.
    \item System identification and state estimation are required to be expressive for representing complex models, capable of modeling uncertainty, and scalable to large data sets. \acfp{DGM}, in particular \acfp{NF}, fulfill these criteria. However, existing architectures must be further developed to adapt to sequential data more flexibly.
    \item By leveraging Bayesian inference to learn control policies and refining them through reinforcement learning, model-free control enables adaptive, uncertainty-aware decision-making in complex, unpredictable environments. This approach has not been fully explored and offers significant potential for advancing control strategies.
\end{itemize}
