\section{State Estimation}
\label{sec:methods:state_estimation}

Adapting normalizing flow methods from parameter identification to state estimation introduces several nontrivial challenges. Unlike parameter identification, which targets a fixed set of unknown variables, state estimation requires the recovery of an entire sequential trajectory from partial observations. In Bayesian terms, the task is to approximate the posterior distribution $p(x_{1:T} | y_{1:T})$, which may be high-dimensional and multimodal. Standard filtering approaches, such as the Kalman filter, typically rely on known linear Gaussian dynamics. The setting considered here imposes no such restrictions: both the state dynamics and the observation model may be nonlinear and stochastic, and the resulting posterior can have a complex geometry. A naive strategy that applies a parameter identification method independently at each time step fails to model temporal dependencies between successive states and allows errors to accumulate over time.

Classical approaches address this problem with sequential Monte Carlo methods, such as particle filters \citep{Nemeth_2014}, which approximate the posterior by propagating a set of weighted particles through time. However, these methods are computationally expensive and suffer from degeneracy in high-dimensional state spaces. Variational approaches, including \acp{VAE} and RNN-based inference networks, have also been proposed for amortized state estimation (see Section~\ref{sec:literature:state_estimation}), but they often struggle to represent complex temporal dependencies and multimodal posteriors. 

To address these limitations, this section introduces four normalizing-flow-based methods for state estimation, ordered by increasing generality: Amortized Sequence Flow (AmortizedSeqFlow), Latent State Space Model Flow (LatentSSMFlow), Normalizing Flow SDE (FlowSDE), and Conditional Stochastic Interpolant (CondStochInterpolant). For AmortizedSeqFlow, we compare RealNVP and Flow Matching as the Bayesian inference backbones. For LatentSSMFlow, an exactly invertible flow is required as the decoder, so RealNVP is implemented. For FlowSDE, the RealNVP architecture is used to model the diffusion term of a NeuralSDE. For CondStochInterpolant, the whole process can be viewed as a refinement flow that transforms an observation-informed source distribution into the posterior state distribution.

All four methods are evaluated on the Lorenz-96 system \citep{lorenz1996predictability}, a canonical benchmark for nonlinear data assimilation. The state dimension is $N = 12$, and at each time step only $N/2 = 6$ components are observed, with additive Gaussian noise of varying magnitude. The governing equations are
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx_i}{dt} = (x_{i+1} - x_{i-2})\,x_{i-1} - x_i + F_l, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, N,
    \label{eq:lorenz96}
\end{equation}
with periodic boundary conditions $x_{-1} = x_{N-1}$, $x_0 = x_N$, $x_{N+1} = x_1$, and forcing constant $F_l = 8$. This forcing regime produces sustained chaotic behavior.

\subsection{Amortized Sequence Flow}\label{sec:amortized_seq_flow}
The most direct approach is to infer states across time by applying a normalizing flow independently to each observation segment. This reduces sequential state estimation to a collection of parameter inference problems. Its main advantage is that the parameter-identification methods introduced in the preceding section can be transferred with minimal modification.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/AmortizedSeqFlow.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture of amortized state estimation with normalizing flows.}
    \label{fig:seq_flow}
\end{figure}
To introduce temporal structure into the latent variables, we use a Wiener process as the base distribution of the normalizing flow. This choice assigns monotonically increasing variance to successive latent variables while preserving a Gaussian distributional form, thereby providing an inductive bias toward smooth trajectories. The architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:seq_flow}.

This simplicity comes at a cost. Because each segment is processed as an independent set of observations, the method largely discards the temporal structure of the state trajectory. Long-range dependencies are not modeled, and estimation errors can propagate across segments without a corrective mechanism. AmortizedSeqFlow is therefore most appropriate when the underlying dynamics are relatively smooth and only weakly dependent over time. It is less suitable when the dynamics are strongly nonlinear or when the observations are substantially noisy.

\paragraph{Numerical Results}
We first evaluate AmortizedSeqFlow under different segment lengths on the partially observed Lorenz-96 system. The dataset contains $2000$ independent trajectories with horizon $[0s,100s]$, time step $dt=0.02s$, and $501$ steps. Each initial state is sampled as a small Gaussian perturbation and then spun up before the training trajectory is recorded. The observation matrix selects every second state coordinate and the others remain unobserved. The observation noise is Gaussian with standard deviation $0.5$. The data are split into $1600$ training trajectories, $200$ validation trajectories, and $200$ test trajectories.

Since this method applies the flow independently to each observation segment, it is not expected to scale well to long windows. The purpose of this experiment is therefore to assess to what extent this simple amortized construction can still be used for state estimation as the segment length increases. Figure~\ref{fig:amortized_seq_flow_segment_metrics} summarizes the quantitative performance, while Figure~\ref{fig:amortized_seq_flow_segment_trajectories} shows representative reconstructed trajectories.

The quantitative results in Figure~\ref{fig:amortized_seq_flow_segment_metrics} show that increasing the segment length decreases the amortized estimation precision. The total RMSE increases monotonically for both architectures. This degradation is mainly caused by the unobserved state components. Thus, the method can interpolate the directly observed coordinates reasonably well, but it becomes increasingly unreliable when reconstructing hidden coordinates over longer windows. Comparing the two architectures, RealNVP has a lower RMSE for the shortest segment length, but its error increases more rapidly than Flow Matching as the segment length grows. The difference results from the characteristics of the two amortized estimators. RealNVP uses an explicitly invertible coupling architecture and is trained through likelihood maximization, which gives it a direct density-estimation objective for each segment. This often makes the posterior samples locally sharper and more stable when the observation window is short. Flow Matching, in contrast, learns a continuous transport vector field from the base process to the state trajectory distribution. Its estimates tend to vary more smoothly along the learned transport path and are more suitable for long-window estimation, but the method is more sensitive to vector-field approximation and numerical integration errors. 

The uncertainty metrics lead to the same conclusion. The $90\%$ \ac{CICP} is close to the nominal level only for short windows; it drops below the nominal level at larger segment lengths. At the same time, the mean credible-interval width increases substantially, especially for Flow Matching. This indicates that the models respond to the harder long-window problem by broadening their posterior uncertainty, but the increased uncertainty is not sufficient to recover consistently calibrated intervals. The calibration KL comparison further suggests that RealNVP matches the uncertainty distribution of the observed components more closely than Flow Matching.

The trajectory reconstructions in Figure~\ref{fig:amortized_seq_flow_segment_trajectories} explain this behavior visually. For segment lengths $5$ and $10$, both methods closely follow the reference trajectory for the shown state components, and the uncertainty bands remain narrow. At segment lengths $20$ and $25$, the estimates are still locally plausible, but deviations become visible around fast transitions and near segment boundaries. The effect is most evident in unobserved states $x[1]$ and $x[3]$, where the reconstructed trajectories begin to drift from the truth and the \acp{CI} widen. RealNVP shows sharper local estimates at short segment lengths, but its long-window reconstructions develop stronger boundary artifacts and larger errors in the unobserved components. Flow Matching is smoother and slightly more robust at the longest segment length, but it also requires wider uncertainty bands. Overall, these results show that AmortizedSeqFlow is usable only for short local state-estimation windows; extending the segment length exposes the limitation of treating segments independently.

\subsection{Latent State Space Model Flow}
\label{sec:methods:latent_ssm_flow}
To overcome the limitation of treating time steps independently, we impose a linear Gaussian \ac{SSM} directly in the latent space of the normalizing flow. This yields the architecture termed LatentSSMFlow, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:latent_ssm_flow}. The model combines a structured temporal prior with a conditional normalizing flow decoder. A further motivation is to build a deep-learning-based Kalman filter or state-space model, as summarized in Sections~\ref{sec:literature:data_assisted_bayes_filter} and~\ref{sec:literature:deep_state_space_model}. In a classical Kalman filter, temporal propagation and uncertainty update are analytically tractable because the transition model is restricted to be linear and the observation model is Gaussian. Here, the linear Gaussian \ac{SSM} preserves this interpretable filtering structure in a latent space, while the flow decoder maps the latent Gaussian process to nonlinear and non-Gaussian physical state distributions. Thus, the model can be viewed as a flow-augmented state-space model that retains tractable temporal propagation and uncertainty updates while relaxing the model restrictions.

\begin{figure}[ht]
   \centering
   \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/LinearSSMFlow.pdf}
   \caption{Architecture of Latent State Space Model Flow.}
   \label{fig:latent_ssm_flow}
\end{figure}

This idea is implemented by changing the base distribution of the flow to a structured dynamical prior. Instead of using an independent standard Gaussian base distribution at each time step, the latent trajectory $z_{1:T}$ follows a Markovian Gaussian process:
\begin{equation}
z_t = F z_{t-1} + \eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,Q).
\end{equation}
The transition matrix $F$ is fixed before training so that the latent dynamics remain contractive or non-expansive. This is enforced by bounding the spectral radius:
\begin{equation*}
    \rho(F) = \max_i |\lambda_i| \le \rho_{max} < 1,
\end{equation*}
where $\lambda_i$ denotes an eigenvalue of $F$. The process noise covariance is written as $Q=LL^\top$, where $L$ is a Cholesky factor. This parameterization guarantees that $Q$ is a valid positive semidefinite covariance matrix. Since the transition matrix $F$ is chosen to be stable, the latent linear Gaussian process has a stationary covariance $P_\infty$ satisfying the discrete Lyapunov equation $P_\infty = F P_\infty F^\top + Q$. We sample the initial latent state from $\mathcal{N}(0,P_\infty)$, so that the latent trajectory starts from the stationary distribution of the base process.

The normalizing flow acts as a nonlinear decoder from the latent state space to the physical state space. At each step, it maps the latent variable $z_t$ to the state $x_t$, conditioned on an observation embedding $c_t$ and the previous state $x_{t-1}$. The conditioning vector $c_t$ is obtained from an encoder applied to the observations. In a filtering setting, this encoder may use only local information such as $y_t$ or $y_{t-1:t}$; in a smoothing setting, it may encode the full observation sequence $y_{1:T}$. Conditioning the flow on $x_{t-1}$ introduces an explicit first-order autoregressive dependence in the decoded state trajectory, while the latent \ac{SSM} provides a global temporal prior.

The likelihood formulation requires an exact invertible decoder. For RealNVP, each state can be transformed into latent space during training because the architecture is bijective:
\begin{equation}
    z_t = f_\phi^{-1}(x_t; c_t, x_{t-1}),\quad c_t=C_\psi(y_{t-1:t}).
\end{equation}
The model is then trained by \ac{MLE}. Using the change-of-variable formula, the log-likelihood of the state sequence is
\begin{equation}
    \log p(x_{1:T} | y_{1:T}) = \log p_{SSM}(z_{1:T}) + \sum_{t=1}^T \log \left| \det \frac{\partial f_\phi(x_t; c_t, x_{t-1})}{\partial x_t} \right|,
\end{equation}
where $p_{SSM}(z_{1:T})$ is the likelihood of the latent trajectory under the linear Gaussian \ac{SSM}. Thus, the latent dynamics are modeled by the \ac{SSM}, while the nonlinear state transition is learned by RealNVP.

Flow Matching, however, is not suitable for an equivalent LatentSSMFlow decoder. Because it has no exact inverse map and no tractable log-determinant, the state trajectory cannot be mapped back to a latent trajectory whose probability is evaluated under $p_{SSM}(z_{1:T})$. Instead, the model is trained with a transport regression objective: samples from the latent \ac{SSM} prior are interpolated toward data states, and the network learns the corresponding velocity field. The latent \ac{SSM} therefore acts only as a source distribution for generation, not as a base measure coupled to the observed state trajectory through an exact change of variables. This distinction causes large training and evaluation mismatches. Thus, Flow Matching is omitted from the LatentSSMFlow comparison.

To improve training efficiency, we use a segmented multiple-shooting strategy. Each trajectory is divided into segments of length $T_s$. Within a segment, the model is unrolled autoregressively, so that prediction errors can influence later states in the same segment. Across segments, the initial state is anchored by the ground-truth state during training, and the segment computations can be parallelized. This strategy keeps the temporal training problem tractable while still exposing the model to multi-step state evolution.

\paragraph{Numerical Results}
We evaluate the RealNVP-based LatentSSMFlow with the same Lorenz-96 dynamics, observation mask, trajectory length, and train-validation-test split as in the previous experiment. The main difference is the observation-noise model. To challenge the uncertainty modeling ability of LatentSSMFlow, the observation noise follows a Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu=5$ degrees of freedom and scale $0.5$. Equivalently, the noise can be written as the Gaussian scale mixture
\begin{equation}
    \tau \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2),\qquad
    e \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I),\qquad
    \eta_t = 0.5\,\frac{e}{\sqrt{\tau}}.
\end{equation}
The marginal noise variance is therefore $\nu(\nu-2)^{-1}0.5^2=5/12\approx0.4167$, corresponding to a marginal standard deviation of approximately $0.646$. This heavier-tailed observation model makes the evaluation more challenging by allowing occasional large observation errors, similar to outlier-contaminated measurements in real systems.

During training, the previous state provided to the decoder is the ground-truth state. In the results shown here, we use models trained with segment lengths $T_s=5,10,20,25,50$. Full-horizon training was not successful. Evaluation is then performed as a continuous autoregressive rollout over the full horizon. At the beginning of a test trajectory, the model is initialized from the given initial state. Thereafter, the previous state provided to the decoder is the model's own previous inferred state. Thus, the rollout tests whether LatentSSMFlow can maintain a coherent state estimate when its predictions are recursively fed back into the model.

The full-horizon rollout evaluation results show that the segment length used during training has a strong effect on long-term consistency. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:latent_ssm_flow_rollout_metrics}, increasing $T_s$ consistently reduces the error over the $100$-step autoregressive rollout. The total RMSE decreases from $2.2$ for $T_s=5$ to $1.1$ for $T_s=50$. The same trend is visible for both observed and unobserved state dimensions, indicating that longer training segments improve the learned latent dynamics rather than only the reconstruction of directly observed coordinates. This behavior contrasts with AmortizedSeqFlow in the previous subsection: here, longer segments expose the autoregressive decoder to longer multi-step dependencies during training and therefore reduce the train--test mismatch in full-horizon rollout.

In terms of uncertainty quantification, the 90\% \ac{CICP} scores remain below the nominal level for all training segment lengths. The \ac{CICP} increases as the segment length grows from $T_s=5$ to $T_s=20$, and then stays relatively stable for $T_s=25$ and $T_s=50$ at approximately $75\%$. For short segments, the model is underfit in the rollout sense: it has only learned local one-step or short-horizon dependencies, so the autoregressive prediction error grows quickly over the full horizon and the posterior intervals cannot cover the reference trajectory. Increasing $T_s$ reduces this dynamic underfitting and improves the posterior mean, which explains the rise in \ac{CICP}. However, the coverage remains far below the nominal $90\%$, indicating that the posterior is still under-dispersed.

The simultaneous decrease in \ac{MCIW} suggests that longer-segment training makes the model more confident as the mean trajectory becomes more accurate. This is beneficial for sharpness, but it also means that the uncertainty does not expand enough to account for residual rollout error. The nearly unchanged KL divergence at $T_s>20$ further supports this interpretation: although the mean prediction improves and the \acp{CI} become narrower, the overall shape mismatch between the learned posterior and the reference uncertainty distribution is not substantially reduced. Thus, increasing the segment length mainly improves deterministic trajectory consistency, while the probabilistic calibration remains limited.

The representative trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:latent_ssm_flow_rollout_trajectories} make the difference between models with different training segment lengths visually clear. Models trained with $T_s=5$ and $T_s=10$ can follow the trajectory locally, but their predictions accumulate errors when rolled out over the full horizon. The divergence is most apparent after major turning points and in the second half of the rollout. By contrast, the models trained with $T_s=20,25,50$ maintain better alignment and reproduce the long-range trajectories more accurately. From the \ac{CI} perspective, longer-segment models cover the true trajectory more often because their mean rollouts are more accurate, even though the intervals themselves become sharper as reflected by the decreasing \ac{MCIW}. 

Figure~\ref{fig:latent_ssm_flow_rollout_uncertainty_distribution} further examines the posterior uncertainty for the $T_s=50$ model. The centered posterior samples are concentrated around their posterior mean, while retaining heavier tails than a narrow Gaussian-like distribution. This indicates that the model is confident for many time--state locations but still assigns probability to larger deviations when the rollout is harder to predict. The posterior standard deviations are mostly below or near the observation-noise scale $0.5$, with only a limited right tail for high-uncertainty regions. The per-state coverage therefore remains below the nominal $90\%$ level, consistent with the aggregate \ac{CICP} of approximately $75\%$. 

Overall, these results reveal a structural limitation of LatentSSMFlow for long-horizon state estimation. Although the latent \ac{SSM} introduces temporal dependence, the decoder is trained mainly through local one-step transitions conditioned on the previous state. This is not sufficient to learn the full long-range dynamics of the partially observed chaotic system. As a result, the model can improve trajectory accuracy when longer training segments are used, but the learned posterior uncertainty remains inadequate: errors accumulated during rollout are not fully reflected in the predictive spread, leading to under-dispersed credible intervals for both observed and unobserved states. 

\subsection{Normalizing Flow SDE}
To further increase the capacity for modeling dynamics and stochasticity, we estimate the state trajectory by representing the underlying dynamics as a stochastic differential equation. We define the conditional state $x_t| y_t$ as a new system variable $z_t$. When the state evolves continuously in time, its dynamics can be written as
\begin{equation}
    dz_t = f(z_t, t)\; dt + g(z_t, t)\;{\color{Orchid} dW_t},\quad dW_t={\color{Orchid} \sqrt{dt}\;\epsilon}, \quad \epsilon\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \boldsymbol{I}).
\end{equation}
By modeling $f$ and $g$ with neural networks, the model can represent nonlinear dynamics and state-dependent noise. However, training a NeuralSDE by directly maximizing the likelihood of the observed data can cause the stochastic component to collapse, since the networks may learn to ignore the diffusion term and fit the dynamics mainly through the drift.

One way to improve the expressiveness of the diffusion term is to use variational inference, as in LatentSDE \citep{li2020scalablegradientsstochasticdifferential}. LatentSDE defines both a prior NeuralSDE and a posterior NeuralSDE, sharing the same diffusion function but using different drift functions. Because the diffusion term is shared, the KL divergence between the two stochastic processes is analytically tractable, allowing the model to be trained by maximizing the \ac{ELBO}. Another approach treats a NeuralSDE as an infinite-dimensional \ac{GAN} \citep{kidger2021neuralsdesinfinitedimensionalgans}. In this framework, the NeuralSDE acts as a generator that transforms Brownian motion into sample paths, while a Neural Controlled Differential Equation (NeuralCDE) acts as a discriminator on continuous-time trajectories. By optimizing a Wasserstein-type objective, this method can match the full distribution of generated paths to the data distribution in path space.

\subsubsection{Integration of Normalizing Flow in NeuralSDE}
Both approaches, however, require substantial computational resources and careful tuning. To reduce this burden, we incorporate a normalizing flow directly into the NeuralSDE framework, avoiding the need for an additional differential equation to support training. For computational efficiency, we use the RealNVP architecture to model the diffusion term. Taking a standard normal random variable $\epsilon\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \boldsymbol{I})$ as the latent variable of the flow gives the following modified SDE:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flow_sde}
    dz_t = f_\varphi(z_t, t)\; dt + g_\phi({\color{Orchid} \epsilon}; z_t, t) \; {\color{Orchid}\sqrt{dt}}, \quad \epsilon\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \boldsymbol{I}),
\end{equation}
where $f_\varphi$ is the drift function and $g_\phi$ is the normalizing flow. 

The factor $\sqrt{dt}$ is included so that the flow-generated random increment has the same time scaling as a standard \ac{SDE} diffusion term, where the variance of the stochastic step scales linearly with time ($\mathcal{O}(dt)$). Decoupling the normalizing flow's input $\epsilon$ from the time step $dt$ avoids this noise collapse and preserves a genuine continuous-time diffusion process.

Unlike standard amortized Bayesian inference, the normalizing flow is not trained by minimizing a KL divergence against an available ground-truth posterior. Instead, the flow is used only in the forward stochastic direction and the latent variable $\epsilon$ is sampled from its standard normal distribution during training. Then, FlowSDE is trained by maximizing the likelihood of the observed data, which requires integrating the SDE (Equation~\ref{eq:flow_sde}) through time. Several SDE solvers can be chosen, such as Euler--Maruyama, Heun, Midpoint, Milstein, or reversible Heun. The initial state is supplied to FlowSDE as part of the initial value problem and is then evolved by the SDE over all state coordinates.

The flow captures complex dependencies between the stochastic input and the current state, enabling the model to learn richer stochastic dynamics. An advantage of RealNVP is its simple Jacobian determinant, which simplifies likelihood computation and supports efficient training. However, RealNVP is non-volume-preserving. To prevent variance collapse, we apply hyperbolic tangent ($\tanh$) clamping to the raw neural network scale output, parameterizing each diagonal scale factor as $\exp(\alpha \cdot \tanh(\cdot))$, where $\alpha > 0$ is a bounding hyperparameter. This restricts the scale factors to the open interval $(e^{-\alpha}, e^{\alpha})$. Thus, RealNVP preserves the capacity to represent expressive, varying noise levels across the state space without suffering from stochastic collapse or gradient explosion.

\subsubsection{Training FlowSDE}
The training objective is formulated probabilistically to prevent noise collapse. Rather than minimizing a deterministic trajectory-matching loss (such as \ac{MSE}), which can force the diffusion term to zero, we minimize the \ac{NLL}. This objective combines the likelihood of the full state trajectory estimated by the SDE with the continuous-time transition likelihood (the normalizing flow loss) of the latent trajectory. Specifically, assuming a Gaussian state likelihood, the state loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{state}}
    =
    \sum_{t} -\log \mathcal{N}\left(x_t; z_t, \sigma_{\text{state}}^2 I\right),
\end{equation}
where $x_t$ is the reference full state and $\sigma_{\text{state}}^2$ controls the assumed state-likelihood variance.

To compute the transition loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{trans}} = \sum_{t} -\log p(z_{t+dt} | z_t)$ under discretization in Equation~\ref{eq:flow_sde}, we apply the change-of-variables formula of normalizing flows:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{trans}} = \sum_{t} \left( \frac{1}{2} \|\epsilon\|^2 + \log \left| \det \frac{\partial g_{\phi}(\epsilon; z_t, t)}{\partial \epsilon} \right| + \frac{D}{2}\log(2\pi dt) \right),
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the dimension of the state space. The standard Gaussian negative log-likelihood of the latent variable $\epsilon$ is a constant. Thus, the transition loss simplifies to the Jacobian-determinant term. It is worth noting that there is a term related to $dt$ in the transition loss, which reflects the time scaling of the diffusion term. For a fixed-step solver, this term is constant and does not affect the optimization. For an adaptive-step solver, since the solver's internal step does not directly affect the network parameters, the gradient of this term is still zero. Unless exact probability density is required, the actual $dt$ used by the solver is not needed. Therefore, the $dt$-term can be treated as a constant during training.

Compared with the previous two methods, FlowSDE is more general: it can represent nonlinear long-term dynamics and state-dependent noise. This expressiveness comes with higher computational cost and more challenging training, since the \ac{SDE} must be integrated while the neural networks are optimized. The overall computational graph for FlowSDE is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:flow_sde_graph}.

We apply a multiple shooting framework to make long-horizon training computationally tractable and more stable. The full trajectory horizon $T$ is divided into $K$ shorter segments of length $T_s=T//K$. On each segment $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, the SDE is integrated independently starting from a given initial state $z_0^{(k)}$. To address trajectory disjointedness, two strategies are integrated into this framework:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuity Constraints:} To force the disjointed segments to form a single continuous trajectory in the continuous-time limit, we add a consistency penalty to the overall training loss:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \| z_{\text{end}}^{(k)} - z_0^{(k+1)} \|^2
    \end{equation}
    where $z_{\text{end}}^{(k)}$ is the terminal state predicted at the end of the $k$-th segment.
    \item \textbf{Longer Horizon Check:} To improve model performance and consistency over longer horizons, we implement a rollout loss within the multiple-shooting framework. For the sake of computational efficiency, the rollout loss is only added to the training loss every 5 optimizer steps. The rollout loss compares the generated trajectories to the true states over the whole trajectory using an energy-score-type objective:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{rollout}} = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(z-x)^2]}+\lambda_u\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(z-z')^2]}.
        \label{eq:flow_sde_rollout_loss}
    \end{equation}
    It consists of two terms: the first term measures the distance between the generated and true states, while the second term penalizes the pairwise spread of the generated trajectories. The hyperparameter $\lambda_u$ controls the uncertainty weight. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item When $\lambda_u=0.5$, the loss corresponds to the standard energy-score scaling and is a strictly proper scoring rule. 
        \item Larger values, such as $\lambda_u=0.8$, place more emphasis on preventing under-dispersed ensembles; this encourages wider trajectories, larger predictive intervals, and higher empirical coverage.
        \item Smaller values, such as $\lambda_u=0.3$, prioritize mean trajectory accuracy and produce narrower intervals.
        \item In the limiting case $\lambda_u=0$, the pairwise spread is ignored, so the rollout loss becomes a deterministic reconstruction objective and the learned stochastic variance can collapse.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

In summary, the overall training objective for FlowSDE combines the state and transition losses with a weighted continuity penalty and a rollout loss.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{state}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{trans}}
    + \lambda_{\text{cont}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{cont}} 
    + \lambda_{\text{rollout}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{rollout}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{*}$ controls the strength of the corresponding term.

\subsubsection{Optional Theoretical Note: Weak Convergence of FlowSDE}
This subsection provides the theoretical justification for the continuous-time limit of FlowSDE.

We first introduce two definitions for Markov-chain convergence \citep{billingsley1999convergence,EthierKurtz1986}.
\begin{definition}[Weak Convergence]
    A sequence of discrete-time Markov chains converges weakly to a diffusion process if the laws of the interpolated processes converge in distribution to the solution of the corresponding stochastic differential equation as $\Delta t \to 0$. 
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[Infinitesimal Generator]
    The infinitesimal generator $\mathcal{A}$ of a Markov chain is an operator that characterizes the expected instantaneous rate of change of a test function along the trajectories of the process. For a discrete-time Markov chain with transition kernel $P_\Delta(z, dz')$, the generator is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{A}h(z) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\int h(z') P_\Delta(z, dz') - h(z)}{\Delta t}
    \end{equation}
    for suitable test functions $h$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Weak Convergence of FlowSDE]
    Let $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be a state variable whose discrete-time temporal transition over a step of size $\Delta t > 0$ is governed by the update rule:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete_update}
        z_{t+\Delta t} = z_t + f_\varphi(z_t, t)\,\Delta t + g_\phi(\epsilon; z_t, t)\,\sqrt{\Delta t}
    \end{equation}
    where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \boldsymbol{I}_d)$ is a standard Gaussian random vector. Assume that:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The drift function $f_\varphi$ and the normalizing flow $g_\phi$ are continuously differentiable and satisfy global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions.
        \item The normalizing flow is centered conditioned on the state, such that $\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[g_\phi(\epsilon; z_t, t)] = 0$ for all $z_t, t$.
        \item The conditional covariance of the flow is defined as:
        \begin{equation}
            \Sigma_\phi(z_t, t) = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[ g_\phi(\epsilon; z_t, t) g_\phi(\epsilon; z_t, t)^T \right]
        \end{equation}
        and has finite second- and higher-order moments.
    \end{itemize}

Then, as the step size $\Delta t \to 0$, the discrete-time sequence defined by \eqref{eq:discrete_update} weakly converges to the unique continuous-time It\^{o} stochastic differential equation:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:continuous_sde}
    dz_t = f_\varphi(z_t, t)\,dt + \Sigma_\phi(z_t, t)^{1/2}\,dW_t
\end{equation}
where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof follows the standard diffusion-approximation argument for locally consistent Markov chains and Euler--Maruyama weak convergence \citep{KushnerDupuis2001,EthierKurtz1986,kloeden1992numerical}. Let
\begin{equation}
    \Delta z_t = z_{t+\Delta t}-z_t
    = f_\varphi(z_t,t)\Delta t
    + g_\phi(\epsilon;z_t,t)\sqrt{\Delta t}.
\end{equation}
For a test function $h\in\mathcal{C}_c^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$, the infinitesimal generator of the limiting process is identified from
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}h(z)
    =
    \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}
    \frac{
        \mathbb{E}\,\left[h(z+\Delta z_t)-h(z)| z_t=z\right]
    }{\Delta t}.
    \label{eq:flow_sde_generator_definition}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Step 1: Taylor expansion.}
A second-order multivariate Taylor expansion around $z$ gives
\begin{equation}
    h(z+\Delta z_t)-h(z)
    =
    \nabla h(z)^T\Delta z_t
    +\frac{1}{2}\Delta z_t^T\nabla^2h(z)\Delta z_t
    +R(\Delta z_t),
    \label{eq:flow_sde_taylor_expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\nabla h(z)$ is the gradient and $\nabla^2h(z)$ is the Hessian of $h$ at $z$. $R(\Delta z_t)$ is the remainder term, which satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    |R(\Delta z_t)| \leq C \cdot |\Delta z_t|^3,
\end{equation}
where $C>0$ is a constant related to the maximum value of the third derivative of $h$.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Step 2: First moment.}
Conditioning on $z_t=z$ and using the centering assumption on the flow $\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)]=0$, the first moment of the increment is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[\Delta z_t| z_t=z]
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\,\left[
        f_\varphi(z,t)\Delta t
        +g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)\sqrt{\Delta t}
    \right] 
    =
    f_\varphi(z,t)\Delta t.
    \label{eq:flow_sde_first_moment}
\end{equation}
Thus the infinitesimal drift is $f_\varphi(z,t)$.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Step 3: Second moment.}
Using $x^TAx=\operatorname{Tr}(Axx^T)$, the quadratic term in
\eqref{eq:flow_sde_taylor_expansion} is determined by
$\mathbb{E}[\Delta z_t\Delta z_t^T| z_t=z]$. Expanding the update gives
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E}[\Delta z_t\Delta z_t^T| z_t=z]
    &=
    f_\varphi(z,t)f_\varphi(z,t)^T\Delta t^2 \notag \\
    &\quad
    +\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\,\left[
        f_\varphi(z,t)g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)^T
        +g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)f_\varphi(z,t)^T
    \right]\Delta t^{3/2} \notag \\
    &\quad
    +\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\,\left[
        g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)^T
    \right]\Delta t.
\end{align}
The cross terms vanish because $\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[g_\phi(\epsilon;z,t)]=0$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[\Delta z_t\Delta z_t^T| z_t=z]
    =
    \Sigma_\phi(z,t)\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2).
    \label{eq:flow_sde_second_moment}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Step 4: Remainder and small jumps.}
The finite higher-order moments of $g_\phi$ imply, for any $p>2$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\,\left[\|\Delta z_t\|^p| z_t=z\right]
    \leq C_p(\Delta t)^{p/2}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the Taylor remainder satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\,\left[|R(\Delta z_t)|| z_t=z\right]
    =\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2}),
    \label{eq:flow_sde_remainder}
\end{equation}
and hence $\mathbb{E}\,\left[|R(\Delta z_t)|| z_t=z\right]/\Delta t\to 0$. This is the Lindeberg-type small-jump condition that excludes a discontinuous jump component in the weak limit.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Step 5: Limiting the infinitesimal generator.}
Substituting the Taylor expansion \eqref{eq:flow_sde_taylor_expansion} into the generator definition \eqref{eq:flow_sde_generator_definition} gives
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{A}h(z)
    &=
    \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}
    \frac{1}{\Delta t}
    \Bigg[
        \nabla h(z)^T
        \mathbb{E}[\Delta z_t| z_t=z] \notag \\
    &\qquad\qquad
        +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\,\left(
            \nabla^2h(z)
            \mathbb{E}[\Delta z_t\Delta z_t^T| z_t=z]
        \right)
        +\mathbb{E}[R(\Delta z_t)| z_t=z]
    \Bigg].
    \label{eq:flow_sde_generator_expansion}
\end{align}
Using the first moment \eqref{eq:flow_sde_first_moment}, the second moment \eqref{eq:flow_sde_second_moment}, and the remainder bound \eqref{eq:flow_sde_remainder}, this becomes
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{A}h(z)
    &=
    \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}
    \frac{1}{\Delta t}
    \Bigg[
        \nabla h(z)^T
        \left(f_\varphi(z,t)\Delta t\right) \notag \\
    &\qquad\qquad
        +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\,\left(
            \nabla^2h(z)
            \left(\Sigma_\phi(z,t)\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\right)
        \right)
        +\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2})
    \Bigg] \notag \\
    &=
    \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}
    \left[
        f_\varphi(z,t)^T\nabla h(z)
        +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\,\left(
            \nabla^2h(z)\Sigma_\phi(z,t)
        \right)
        +\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
        +\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{1/2})
    \right] \notag \\
    &=
    f_\varphi(z,t)^T\nabla h(z)
    +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\,\left(
        \Sigma_\phi(z,t)\nabla^2h(z)
    \right).
    \label{eq:flow_sde_limiting_generator}
\end{align}
To connect this operator with a continuous-time SDE, recall that an It\^{o}
diffusion of the form
\begin{equation}
    dZ_t=b(Z_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(Z_t,t)\,dW_t
\end{equation}
has infinitesimal generator
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}h(z)
    =
    b(z,t)^T\nabla h(z)
    +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\,\left(
        \sigma(z,t)\sigma(z,t)^T\nabla^2h(z)
    \right).
\end{equation}
Comparing this standard form with \eqref{eq:flow_sde_limiting_generator}
shows that
\begin{equation}
    b(z,t)=f_\varphi(z,t),
    \qquad
    \sigma(z,t)\sigma(z,t)^T=\Sigma_\phi(z,t).
\end{equation}
Therefore, choosing $\sigma(z,t)=\Sigma_\phi(z,t)^{1/2}$ gives the limiting
It\^{o} SDE
\begin{equation}
    dz_t
    =
    f_\varphi(z_t,t)\,dt
    +\Sigma_\phi(z_t,t)^{1/2}\,dW_t.
\end{equation}
Thus, the discrete update has, in the limit $\Delta t\to 0$, the same
infinitesimal generator as this It\^{o} diffusion. By the standard generator
convergence theorem for Markov processes, the Markov-chain approximation
converges weakly to the stated continuous-time SDE.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Conditional Stochastic Interpolant}
The preceding methods apply normalizing flow architectures to model state trajectories. Stochastic Interpolants \citep{albergo2025si} provide a different perspective, as introduced in Section~\ref{sec:background:si}. Instead of inferring states from observations, we formulate state estimation as signal-to-signal refinement: an observed trajectory $y_{1:T}$ is gradually transformed into samples that follow the posterior trajectory distribution $p(x_{1:T}| y_{1:T})$. The gradual refinement is parameterized by an artificial flow from time $\tau=0$ to $\tau=1$, and this transformation is modeled as a stochastic process.

\subsubsection{Conditional Stochastic Interpolant for State Estimation}
In the classical generative setting, an interpolant connects a simple source distribution, such as a Gaussian prior, to the data distribution. For state estimation, the observations should constrain the generative process as conditioning information. We therefore define a conditional stochastic interpolant by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{U}_0 \sim p_0(\cdot | y_{1:T}), \qquad
    \mathcal{U}_1 \sim p(x_{1:T}| y_{1:T}),
\end{equation}
where $p_0(\cdot | y_{1:T})$ is an observation-informed source distribution. The source distribution is obtained by an inference network $q_\eta$:
\begin{equation}
    \left[\mu_\eta,\,\log \sigma_\eta^2\right]
    =
    q_\eta(y_{1:T}).
    \label{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_encoder}
\end{equation}
The parameters $\mu_\eta$ and $\sigma_\eta$ are reparameterized to build the source distribution, which guarantees differentiability and allows gradients to propagate back to the inference network:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{U}_0 = \mu_\eta + \sigma_\eta \odot \epsilon_0,
    \qquad
    \epsilon_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \boldsymbol{I}).
    \label{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_source}
\end{equation} 

This inference network provides an adaptive initialization for the interpolant, which can be trained end-to-end with the interpolant dynamics. The source distribution is not fixed as a simple prior; instead, it is learned to capture the information in the observations and to provide a good starting point for the refinement process.

To better extract information from observations, a conditioning network $C_\psi$ is applied:
\begin{equation}
    c = C_\psi(y_{1:T}).
\end{equation} 
The conditioning information $c$ is fed into the interpolant dynamics, which allows the refinement process to be directly informed by the observations at every step along the flow time $\tau$.

\paragraph{Target Interpolant}
The target interpolant can be defined using either a simple linear path or a smoother cosine path. For the default linear interpolant, we use
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{U}_\tau = \alpha(\tau)\,\mathcal{U}_0 + \beta(\tau)\,\mathcal{U}_1 + \sigma_\tau\epsilon,
    \qquad
    \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
    \label{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_path}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\tau)=1-\tau,\qquad \beta(\tau)=\tau,\qquad
    \sigma_\tau=\sigma_0\tau(1-\tau).
\end{equation}
The corresponding derivatives are
\begin{equation}
    \alpha'(\tau)=-1,\qquad \beta'(\tau)=1,\qquad
    \dot{\sigma}(\tau)=\sigma_0(1-2\tau),
\end{equation}
which define the supervised target velocity
\begin{equation}
    v^{*}
    =
    \alpha'(\tau)\,\mathcal{U}_0+\beta'(\tau)\,\mathcal{U}_1+\dot{\sigma}(\tau)\epsilon.
    \label{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_velocity_target}
\end{equation}
For the linear path, this target is the instantaneous direction of the noisy bridge from the source trajectory $\mathcal{U}_0$ to the target state trajectory $\mathcal{U}_1$.

\paragraph{Learned Interpolant Dynamics}
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/CondStochInterpolant.pdf}
    \caption[Computational graph of Conditional Stochastic Interpolant]{Computational graph of Conditional Stochastic Interpolant. The inference network $q_\eta$ produces the source distribution parameters $(\mu_\eta, \sigma_\eta)$ from the observations $y_{1:T}$. The conditioning network $C_\psi$ extracts conditioning information $c$ from the observations. Both the mean parameter $\mu_\eta$ and the conditioning information $c$ are used as conditions for the transport velocity network $v_\varphi$ and the score network $S_\phi$, which model the deterministic and stochastic components of the interpolant dynamics, respectively. The stochastic solver integrates the interpolant \ac{SDE} forward in flow time $\tau$, starting from the source trajectory $\mathcal{U}_0$, to produce terminal trajectories that approximate samples from the posterior distribution over state trajectories.}
    \label{fig:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_graph}
\end{figure}
The learned interpolant dynamics are described by a refinement \ac{SDE} with a deterministic transport term and a score-based correction:
\begin{equation}
    d\mathcal{U}_\tau =
    \left[
        v_\varphi(\mathcal{U}_\tau, \tau; \mu_\eta, c)
        +g_0^2 S_\phi(\mathcal{U}_\tau,\tau; \mu_\eta, c)\right]d\tau
    + g_0\sqrt{2\sigma_\tau}dW_\tau,
    \label{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_general}
\end{equation}
where both neural fields, the transport velocity $v_\varphi$ and the score network $S_\phi$, are conditioned on the context $c$ and on the encoder mean $\mu_\eta$. The encoder mean $\mu_\eta$ provides a state-space estimate of the trajectory and gives the SDE a physically meaningful state-space reference. 

The model is trained end-to-end with a joint encoder-interpolant objective:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}}
    +\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}
    +\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{nll}},
    \label{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_loss}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}}
    &=
    \left\|
        v_\varphi(\mathcal{U}_\tau,\tau;\mu_\eta,c)-v^*
    \right\|^2, \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}
    &=
    \left\|
        S_\phi(\mathcal{U}_\tau,\tau;\mu_\eta,c)+\epsilon
    \right\|^2, \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{nll}}
    &=
    -\log
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(
        x_{1:T};
        \mu_\eta,
        \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\eta^2)
    \right).
\end{align}
The flow loss learns the deterministic transport field, while the score loss trains the score network in a sigma-scaled form. The negative log-likelihood term trains the inference network to assign high probability to the reference state trajectory $x_{1:T}$ under the Gaussian source distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu_\eta,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\eta^2))$.

Since $\mathcal{U}_\tau$ and $v^*$ depend on the sampled source $\mathcal{U}_0$ (see Equations~\ref{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_velocity_target} and~\ref{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_path}), the flow and score losses are differentiable with respect to $\mathcal{U}_0$. Consequently, gradients from the losses propagate through the interpolant path into the inference network $q_\eta$.

For a new observation trajectory, posterior estimation is performed by evaluating the inference network once, drawing $N_p$ source particles from Equation~\ref{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_source} using independent samples of $\epsilon_0$, solving Equation~\ref{eq:conditional_stochastic_interpolant_general} with an Euler--Maruyama discretization, and collecting the terminal particles $\{u_1^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_p}$. These terminal trajectories represent samples from the approximate posterior $p(x_{1:T}| y_{1:T})$.

CondStochInterpolant therefore acts as a Bayesian filter/smoother. Based on the observed data, the stochastic inference network produces an adaptive initial particle distribution, and the refinement \ac{SDE} transports those particles into a calibrated and potentially multimodal posterior over full state trajectories.

This indicates that the model uses the refinement \ac{SDE} as a denoising and data-generation mechanism rather than directly interpolating noisy measurements.

\subsection{Summary of State Estimation Methods}

Across the four methods, a clear trade-off emerges between reconstruction accuracy, uncertainty quantification, and the ability to capture long-range temporal structure. AmortizedSeqFlow is the simplest and most computationally efficient approach, and it performs reasonably accurately for short local windows. However, because it treats each segment independently, it is less effective when the horizon becomes longer or when the dynamics are strongly nonlinear. LatentSSMFlow improves upon this by introducing a structured latent temporal prior, which helps preserve temporal consistency and leads to better long-horizon rollouts than AmortizedSeqFlow. Nevertheless, its ability to model system dynamics is limited, and the uncertainty estimates remain under-dispersed.

FlowSDE provides a more expressive framework for modeling nonlinear dynamics and stochasticity. It achieves strong trajectory reconstruction and more meaningful uncertainty calibration because the flow-based diffusion term allows the model to represent both dynamical uncertainty and multimodal posterior structure. CondStochInterpolant treats state estimation as a refinement process from an observation-informed source distribution to the posterior trajectory distribution; although it cannot yield the most precise point estimates because of its stochastic procedure, it provides a flexible and expressive posterior estimator that captures multimodal uncertainty and preserves temporal coherence under partially observed, non-Gaussian noise.
